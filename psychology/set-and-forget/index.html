Why you can't set and forget anymore, and how to get it back

When I started trading in 2010, I'd place a trade, set the stop and target, and get on with my day. In 2026 I catch myself watching every candle. So is it the market making me neurotic, or has the game really changed? Honestly, it's a bit of both. This is what changed, why watching makes it worse, and what I'm doing to get my set-and-forget back.

The market isn't the one I learned on

You're not imagining it. Since 2020 the market has felt different, and there are good reasons why.

2010
  • News moved price, then it settled. You had a few minutes to read the number before the market made up its mind.
  • Ranges were steadier. A stop sized from last month still made sense this month.
  • You checked the trade at lunch, maybe after work. There was no phone pinging in your pocket all day.
  • A stop below the obvious low usually survived. Fewer people knew where everyone else's stops were.
2026
  • Algorithms read the headline before you do. The first move after news happens in milliseconds, and it often reverses just as fast.
  • Ranges have widened and keep changing. The pandemic, the inflation shock, the fastest rate hikes in decades, tariffs and wars: volatility comes in waves now.
  • Liquidity thins out at key moments. Around news and the rollover, price can jump through a level instead of trading through it.
  • Stop hunts are sharper. Everyone can see the obvious levels and the stop loss clusters, so a stop sitting on one gets taken.
  • The chart is always in your pocket. Every tick is one tap away, and that changes how we behave.

So is it me, or the market?

Both, and it helps to split them, because each half needs a different fix.

The market's half

The old settings don't fit

A stop sized for 2010's ranges will get tagged more often in 2026's. That isn't you being weak, it's maths. If your stops and size haven't moved with the market, the trade really is fragile, and part of you knows it. That's why you watch.

Our half

Watching feels like control

When the swings are wild, staring at the screen feels like doing something about it. It isn't. Your stop and target were already doing the job. Watching only adds decisions, and decisions made mid-trade are usually the worst ones we make.

The honest test

Ask yourself: "If I couldn't see this trade until it closed, would I still take it?" If the answer is no, the setup or the size is wrong, and no amount of watching will fix it. If the answer is yes, you don't need to watch.

What babysitting actually costs

Watching a trade doesn't change what price does. It changes what you do. These are the habits it breeds, and I've been guilty of every one.

✕
Cutting winners earlyThe trade is up 1R, then gives back half. You grab what's left "before it's gone", and it goes on to hit the target without you. Do that a few times and a good system starts to look like a bad one.
✕
Moving the stopEither too tight, too soon, right where the next wick will take it, or further away to "give it room", which turns a 1R loss into a 2R one.
✕
Reacting to the first move on newsThe algorithms' first spike is often the fake one. Closing or flipping into it means you trade their noise, not your plan.
✕
Trading out of boredomHours at the screen with nothing to do invites a second trade, and a third. Most of them were never in the plan.
✕
Burning outYour attention is limited. Spend it all watching one open trade and there's nothing left for the next good setup, or for the rest of your life.

How to get set-and-forget back

The goal isn't to become a calmer person overnight. It's to build trades that don't need you watching them. Each step below takes a decision away from the moment and puts it in the plan.

Size the stop to today's market, not 2010'sUse the pair's average daily range (ADR) or the ATR to see how far price normally moves, and put the stop beyond the strong level and beyond the crowd's stop cluster sitting on it. Never on a round number, never on the obvious low.If EURUSD moves 80 pips a day, a 15-pip stop is a coin toss.📏 Find each pair's ADR, and how much of today's range is already used, on the free Analytics dashboards in the “Previous day range & ADR” panel.
Trade smaller, so the wider stop costs the sameA wider stop doesn't mean more risk if the position is smaller. Keep each loss to the same 0.5 to 1% of the account. The swings feel much less wild when less is riding on them.$10,000 account, 1% risk = $100. 20-pip stop → 0.5 lots. 40-pip stop → 0.25 lots. Same $100 if it's wrong.
Plan around the news, don't watch through itCheck the calendar before you enter. If a central bank decision or a big data release is due while the trade is open, be flat, reduce size, or wait until after. A clean setup into a rate decision is a coin flip.
Write the management rules before you enterDecide now what you'll do at each stage, so there's nothing to decide while you're watching. For example: move the stop to break-even at 1R, take a partial at the first magnet, let the rest run to target. If the rule isn't written down, you don't do it.
Set alerts, not screen timePut price alerts at your entry, at 1R and at the level that would prove you wrong. Then close the chart and walk away on purpose. If the alert hasn't gone off, nothing needs you.
Pick your check-in timesTwo or three set times a day, such as the London open, the New York open and before bed. Outside them, the trade looks after itself. If you manage the trade on a schedule, you're trading. If you manage it on a feeling, you're babysitting.

My bad habit: micro-managing

I'll be honest: I micro-manage. I nudge the stop, take a bit off early, step in "just in case", and I tell people in the Discord when I do it. Now and then it saves a trade. More often it costs me the move the plan was built for, and the "reason" I found was one I invented while watching. So this whole piece is me talking to myself too: set and forget is the goal, and every fix above is there to take my hands off the trade.

The one exception is scalping and intraday trading, where managing the trade is the system and the rules are written in advance. For the rest of us, the test is simple: if the plan didn't say to touch it, don't.

A note from me

I run a Discord full of traders, and I still struggle with this. I don't think that makes me less qualified to talk about it. I think it's the reason to. Nobody beats the psychology side once and for all. You build a process that protects you from yourself, and you keep coming back to it.

If you've been babysitting your trades since 2020, you're not broken and you're not alone. The market got faster. Our plans need to catch up with it, so that we can step away again.

The one rule

Never trade sentiment on its own. Use it only together with technical and fundamental analysis, and with strict risk management. A trade built that way is a trade you can walk away from.
