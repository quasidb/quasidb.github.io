The Elephant and the Rider: headlines, the herd and your emotions

There's an old picture of the mind that every trader should know. Your emotions are an elephant: huge, fast and powerful. Your reasoning is the rider sitting on top, holding the reins. On a calm day the rider steers. But when the elephant gets spooked, the rider is just along for the ride, and that's when the plan gets trampled.

The elephant and the rider

The psychologist Jonathan Haidt made this picture famous in his book The Happiness Hypothesis, building on much older Buddhist imagery of the mind as an elephant that has to be tamed.

The elephant

Emotion

Fear, greed, fight or flight. It reacts in an instant, it's far stronger than the rider, and it doesn't read the fine print.

In trading, it's the part of you that slams the close button, doubles the size, or chases the candle.

The rider

Reasoning

Slow, logical and able to plan ahead. It knows about levels, stops and 1% risk.

But it can't out-muscle the elephant. It can only steer, and only if it trained the elephant before the trip.

You'll sometimes hear this put as "left brain vs right brain". That split is a popular myth. Emotion vs reasoning is the useful part, and it's all you need.

Headlines are written to make you feel something

Most people don't read the article, let alone the fine print. They read the headline, and headlines are written to make you feel something, because feelings get clicks. Fear, anger and outrage work best, and those feelings tend to keep people defensive and bearish.

Here's the bit that matters for us. You're not the only one reading it. Thousands of traders read the same headline at the same moment, feel the same thing, and press the same button. That's how a headline swings sentiment, and how the herd ends up on the same side, with its stops in the same place.

Why this helps a contrarian

When the crowd reacts to a headline, you can see it in the positioning. That's exactly what the QDB Sheet and the free sentiment pages track: who just piled in, and which way. The elephant moves the herd. The data shows you the herd.

The classic: the dot-com bubble

In the late 1990s everyone got super bullish on tech. The story was that anyone could set up a website and make it rich. Headlines fed the euphoria, the crowd piled in, and the Nasdaq went parabolic.

Approximate shape of the Nasdaq Composite, 1995 to 2003. The headlines are paraphrased moods of the time, not quotes.

From its peak in March 2000 to its low in October 2002, the Nasdaq lost almost 80% of its value. My take: the euphoria was the catalyst, but those stocks were also ripe for taking profit. It was most likely a combination of both, and the herd was buying right at the top.

Records with wars going on?

Here's a question I get asked: why do the indices hit all-time highs while wars are going on and the news is full of doom? If the headlines were in charge, they couldn't.

The honest answer is that the market prices money, not mood: liquidity, interest rates and what companies are expected to earn. Headlines can shake prices for a day or a week. But when the money keeps flowing in, the trend wins, and the traders who sold the scary headline become the fuel for the next leg up.

The lesson

The headline tells you how people feel. The chart and the positioning tell you what they're doing. Trade the second one.

How to train the rider

You can't get rid of the elephant, and you wouldn't want to. You just need to train it before the trip, not argue with it during the stampede.

Make the decisions while the elephant is asleepYour level, your stop beyond the crowd's cluster, your size at 0.5 to 1% and your target. All written down before you open the news.
Name the feelingWhen a headline hits, say it out loud: "that's fear" or "that's greed". Naming it gives the rider a second to pick up the reins.
Never trade the headlineWait for the first spike to settle. The algorithms trade the headline in milliseconds, and the first move is often the fake one.
Check the herdIf the crowd is piling in the same way the headline is pushing, be careful. That's usually where the stops and the reversals are.
Let the plan decideIf the plan didn't say to act on news, you don't. The elephant can stamp its feet all it likes.

A note from me

It's very, very common for traders to let the elephant take control. I've done it myself, more times than I'd like to admit, usually right after reading a headline that made me angry or scared.

The rider never wins a fight with the elephant. It wins by planning the route before the elephant wakes up. That's all a trading plan really is.

The one rule

Never trade sentiment on its own. Use it only together with technical and fundamental analysis, and with strict risk management.
